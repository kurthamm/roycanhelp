# Roy Can Help, the podcast: things only Roy can decide

These are the open questions. Kurt and I have opinions on some of them, noted
where we have them, but none of this is settled until Roy says so. It is his
show and his name on it.

---

## 1. Does he actually want to do this?

Twenty minutes of audio is a couple of hours of work the first few times. Before
anything else: record one episode, alone, and see whether he enjoys it. No
planning session will answer this. A pilot script is already written to make that
first try as easy as possible.

**Kurt and I lean:** try one episode from a question he has already answered on
the site, then decide.

---

## 2. What is the show called, and what is the one-line description?

"Roy Can Help" matches the site, which is worth a lot. Podcast apps show a short
description under the title, and it is the only sales pitch most people ever
read.

Working line to react to: *What the system owes your kid, and how to make it pay
up.*

Does he want the show tied tightly to the site name, or does the podcast get its
own identity?

---

## 3. Solo, co-host, or both?

Roy alone is the product. Guests occasionally. That was the starting instinct and
it is a good one.

Open questions:
- Does he want a regular co-host eventually, someone to react to and argue with?
  It makes recording easier and less lonely, but it means scheduling around
  another person forever.
- If not a co-host, does he want a recurring voice? A parent who calls in, a
  friend who plays the skeptic?

**Kurt and I lean:** start solo. Add a co-host later only if the solo episodes
feel flat to him. Never lock it in before he knows what the show is.

---

## 4. How angry, and at whom?

The register we landed on: quiet and precise, not loud. Fair to the people
inside the system, hard on the design of it. Read the regulation aloud and let
the document do the shouting.

The reframe that matters: not "they are hiding this from you," but "nobody's job
is to make sure you find it." It is more defensible, more damning, and it keeps
state and federal people willing to come on the show.

Open questions for Roy:
- Does that sound like him, or is it a costume?
- Where is his actual limit? What makes him genuinely angry versus what he can
  discuss calmly?
- Is he willing to be fair to a district on tape when he does not feel fair?

---

## 5. Format and length

Proposed shape, to accept or tear up:
- Cold open with the real question or story, no music first
- The fair hearing: the honest case for the other side
- The turn: where patience ends
- The rule, read aloud, with the citation
- What to say: the exact words to use
- Close: the takeaway and where the written version lives

Open questions:
- How long? Twenty minutes is the recommendation. Sixty is a different show and
  a different commitment.
- Does he want recurring segments? The Mad Magazine move fits here: what they
  say, next to what it means. That could run every episode forever.
- Music, or no music?

---

## 6. Cadence

Solo podcasts die on cadence, not on quality. Every other week, kept up for a
year, beats an hour whenever inspiration strikes.

What can he actually sustain in a bad month, not a good one? That is the real
schedule.

---

## 7. Guests

Kinds worth having:
- State and federal program people (BabyNet, DDSN, SC DHHS, and the federal
  equivalents). The pitch to them: their outreach budget is small, this audience
  is exactly who they are trying to reach, and he will not ambush them.
- A special education attorney
- A parent whose child is now an adult, who has been through the whole arc
- A school psychologist willing to say what really happens in evaluations
- Family Connection of South Carolina

Open questions:
- Does he want guests at all, or does that dilute the thing that makes it his?
- Interview style: friendly and curious, or press conference?
- Does a guest episode replace a solo episode that week, or add to the schedule?
  (Replacing is the only sustainable answer.)

---

## 8. South Carolina or national?

The site is national with a South Carolina file. The podcast could go either way.
Local is more useful and more specific. National is a bigger audience and vaguer
advice.

**A middle path:** national law, South Carolina examples. The rules are federal
anyway. The waitlists and phone numbers are local.

---

## 9. What he will not do

Worth deciding once, up front, so he never has to decide in the moment:
- Naming specific districts, schools, or employees. Recommended answer: never.
  Name the practice, not the place. It is the better bit and it is not a lawsuit.
- Giving medical or legal advice. Same rule as the site.
- Talking about his son's specifics. How much of his own kid's story is on the
  table is entirely his call, and he should decide before a guest asks him on
  tape.
- Ads, sponsors, and money. Does he want this to stay free and unsponsored like
  the site? Disability-adjacent advertisers will eventually come knocking.

---

## 10. Gear and logistics

- Does he have a decent microphone and a quiet room, or is step one buying gear?
  A usable setup is not expensive, but phone audio will sound like phone audio.
- Who edits? Editing is the part that kills most podcasts. Light editing, or none
  at all, is a legitimate choice for a show like this.
- Where does it live? The episodes can be hosted on his own site with a feed that
  Apple and Spotify read, no monthly hosting fee, and he could publish by
  uploading the audio through the chat he already uses. That is a build decision
  for later, not something he needs to think about now.

---

## The short version

Two questions before anything else happens:

1. Will he record one episode?
2. Does the voice we described (quiet, precise, fair to people and hard on the
   system) sound like him?

Everything else can wait until after episode one exists.
