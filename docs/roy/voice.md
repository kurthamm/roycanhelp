# Roy's Voice: A Guide for Writers

## How Roy Sounds (8-10 Sentences)

Roy is a dad and engineer who writes like someone who has spent years in waiting rooms and IEP meetings and lived to tell the tale. He speaks in first person, directly to parents as peers, never down to them. His tone is firm and calm, irreverent toward bureaucracy (never toward people or disability), and honest about both the system's failures and his own. He addresses readers as collaborators who have to figure this out—not as victims needing inspiration, not as people who have failed, but as people in a hard situation who deserve a spec sheet instead of a pep talk. He protects his son and his family fiercely while being clear about what he doesn't know. He values precision about rules (he cites regulations), honesty about costs and traps, and playful humor about the absurdity of forms and waiting lists. He writes in short, direct sentences mixed with longer ones that layer detail. He never sounds combative or angry; he sounds like someone who reads regulations the way other people read sports stats, who knows the system is broken but treats it as a system to debug, not an enemy to fight.

## His Recurring Structures and Patterns

### Section Headings and Frameworks

Roy uses consistent structural patterns that guide readers through information:

- **"What This Actually Is"** - He defines terms by what they really do, not what the acronym says. Example: "Early Intervention (Part C) is free. I mean actually, truly free. No insurance required." He uses this to cut through jargon.

- **"How to Get In" / "How to Request"** - Step-by-step numbered lists starting with the easiest action. Always actionable, never theoretical.

- **"Traps I Fell Into"** - Personal mistakes reframed as lessons, always honest about what he did wrong and what he learned. This removes shame and builds trust.

- **"[Condition] in Early Intervention / School / Adult Services"** - He provides disability-specific guidance (Down syndrome, autism) while emphasizing that his main experience is autism and South Carolina.

- **"Your Next Step"** - Closes every page with the single most important action and where to find state-specific resources.

- **"Timeline: What to Do When"** - Age-based checkpoints for major decisions (e.g., Age 14-15, Age 17-18, Age 26).

### Sentence Length and Rhythm

Roy mixes sentence types strategically:

- **Short, punchy declarations:** "Once you know the spec, you can debug it."
- **Medium sentences with precise detail:** "The school has 45 days from the date they receive your referral to complete the evaluation, do initial assessments, hold an IFSP meeting, and have a signed plan in place."
- **Longer sentences packed with parenthetical asides:** "(not a lawyer, not a doctor, but someone who spent years learning the system)"

### Parenthetical Asides

Roy uses parentheses for:
- Quick humor: "(I'm that old)" / "(yes, I'm that old)"
- Honest interjections: "(which was a mistake)"
- Technical clarifications: "(also called a supplemental needs trust)"
- Self-deprecating observations: "(I thought I was fine while my hands shook)"

### Lists

His lists are:
- **Always numbered when steps matter** (how to request evaluation, timeline of what to do when)
- **Always bulleted when options or traps are equal weight** (things to avoid, multiple programs)
- **Each item starts with a strong verb or noun phrase**, not a dependent clause
- **Each item is complete and standalone** — you could read item 3 without items 1-2

### People-First Language Choices

Roy:
- Says "a child with autism," "a child with Down syndrome" (never "autistic child" or "Down's child")
- Says "child with a disability," not "disabled child"
- Says "developmental delay," not "delayed development"
- Uses "person with a disability" for adults
- Never says "child" when he means a specific person — he says "my son" when it's personal, "[child's name]" in examples

### Contractions and Formality

Roy uses:
- Contractions in normal prose ("I'm a dad, not a doctor")
- Formal regulatory language when citing rules ("34 CFR 303.303")
- Conversational but never sloppy ("your child," not "your kid"; never "your youngster")

### How He References Regulations

Roy:
- **Cites the actual regulation** (42 USC 1382b, 34 CFR 303.310) so readers can look it up
- **Explains what the regulation means in plain English first**, then gives the citation
- **Never assumes readers know the regulation system** but assumes they're smart enough to read it if given the citation
- **Links to ssa.gov, ecfr.gov, and official government sources**, never to private advice sites

## First-Person Safety Rules: What's Safe to Write in Roy's Voice vs. Not Safe

### SAFE: First-Person Statements Roy or New Authors Can Write

These kinds of first-person writing are safe to attribute to Roy or use in his voice:

- **Opinions about approach:** "Here is what I would check first." "I would start by calling the state office." "I think it's important to get everything in writing."
- **Reactions to broken systems:** "This frustrated me so much." "I was angry when I realized..." "I couldn't believe they..."
- **Honest limits:** "I don't know anything about [state] because I'm from South Carolina." "This is outside my wheelhouse." "My experience is autism and Special Education, not medical complexity."
- **What he would ask for:** "The questions I would ask are..." "Here's what I would want in writing..." "I would push back on..."
- **What he would do next:** "Next, I would..." "The first call I would make..." "Before signing anything, I would..."
- **Practical tips from his experience:** "From my experience, this works..." "When I did this, it took..." "What I've seen is..."

### NOT SAFE: First-Person Statements That Must Not Be Attributed to Roy

These are dangerous because they make claims about his life, family, or specific past events that Roy hasn't published:

- **Any event, date, or number about his son or family** not already in his published pages. Do not write "My son was diagnosed at 18 months" or "We waited 14 months for therapy" unless Roy wrote that exact fact. His son's story is autism, medical complexity, and his age is implicit in the timeline, but specific details of his life are his to tell, not for others to invent.

- **Feelings about his son** beyond what's already stated. Roy has written about grief being real, love being love, and shame not being his. He hasn't written about what he felt the day of diagnosis beyond "I honestly can't remember what I said after that." Do not invent his emotional journey.

- **Specific numbers or money** related to his family's situation. Do not write "We paid $X for therapy" or "SSI denied us and we struggled for Y years" unless Roy wrote it.

- **Personal beliefs or faith** beyond what's in the guidelines. Kurt noted Roy is a "regular churchgoer and a solid believer," but Roy's faith appears on the site only in words Roy himself writes or approves. Do not write prayer-like language, religious framing, or faith statements in his voice.

- **Specific provider recommendations or criticisms.** Roy doesn't recommend specific therapy companies, schools, or doctors by name. He recommends systems, programs, and questions to ask, not people.

- **Political opinions or crusades.** Roy is firm about his rights and his son's rights, but he doesn't rail against "the system is broken" as a political statement. He treats the system as something to navigate, debug, and know.

## Do and Do Not List for New First-Person Content

### DO:

- Start with a real situation or question Roy has faced or that parents ask him
- Use his exact language patterns (short sentences, parentheticals, "Specs beat opinions")
- Include a regulation citation and link if making a legal claim
- Be honest about what Roy doesn't know (South Carolina-only, autism-only, education-focused)
- Use numbered steps for "how to" advice and bulleted lists for options
- Include a "traps I would avoid" or "what I learned" section if it fits the page
- Close with "Your Next Step" and state-specific resource pointers
- Cite Roy by name when telling a story ("Roy learned...") but only with his permission via preview

### DO NOT:

- Invent anecdotes about his son or family
- Assume Roy's feelings beyond what he's written
- Use invented fun facts or research claims ("studies show")
- Write religious or faith-based framing without Roy's explicit approval
- Recommend specific providers or therapy companies
- Use combative language ("fight the school," "battle the system")
- Write gratuitous humor or sarcasm (humor is fine; meanness is not)
- Make claims about money, timelines, or specific numbers from his life
- Write in second person as if you are Roy without explicit review and approval
- Remove or soften his strong language about the system being broken (he said it; it's fair)

He uses short sentences, parenthetical humor, regulation citations, and honest admissions of his own mistakes. He speaks with firm calm, irreverent toward bureaucracy, protective of his son, and clear about his limits (South Carolina, autism, engineer).
