# Roy Can Help, Pilot Episode
## "Let's Wait and See"

Target length: 18 to 22 minutes. Timings are guides, not rules.

Voice notes for Roy: quiet and precise, not loud. Read regulations aloud slowly
and let the document do the shouting. Concede every fair excuse before the turn.
The anger is at the design of the system, never at the people working inside it.
Nobody is hiding this from parents. It is just sitting there, and no one's job is
to make sure they find it.

---

### COLD OPEN (0:00 to 1:00)

No music yet. Straight in.

> A mom emails me last month. Her son is three and a half. Barely talking.
> She asks the school to test him. The school says, and I want you to hear the
> exact words, because you are going to hear them too: "Let's wait and see."
>
> She waited. She waited eleven months. And when they finally tested him, he
> qualified. He had qualified the whole time.
>
> Those eleven months were not a delay. They were services he was legally
> entitled to and did not get. Nobody broke the law telling her to wait. That is
> what I want to talk about today.

Theme music, then intro.

### INTRO (1:00 to 1:45)

> This is Roy Can Help. I am Roy. My son has autism, I am an engineer by
> training, and I have spent years learning that the special education system is
> not a mystery. It is a system. It has rules, it has timelines, and most of them
> are written down where you can read them.
>
> Every episode I take one question a parent actually sent me and we go through
> what the law says you are owed. Not what is nice. Not what the district can
> afford. What is owed.

### THE FAIR HEARING (1:45 to 6:00)

Give the other side its best case. This makes the turn land.

Points to hit:
- Some kids really are late talkers, and a good teacher has seen that.
- Evaluations take staff the district may genuinely not have. School
  psychologists are carrying enormous caseloads.
- The person saying "wait and see" is usually not lying and often believes it.
- Districts have real budget limits, and every evaluation has a cost attached.

> I want to be fair here, because if I am not fair you should not trust anything
> else I tell you. The teacher who says give it time is usually not trying to
> cheat your kid. She has thirty years of watching late talkers turn out fine.
> The district has one school psychologist for four schools. None of that is
> made up.
>
> And none of it changes what the law requires.

### THE TURN (6:00 to 7:00)

Voice drops here. This is the signature beat.

> Here is where my patience ends. Not with the teacher. With the design.
>
> The system is built so that if you know the rule, you get the evaluation, and
> if you do not know the rule, you get "let's wait and see." Same child. Same
> need. Different outcome, based entirely on whether the parent happened to know
> something nobody told them.
>
> That is not a conspiracy. It is worse than a conspiracy, because there is
> nobody to catch. It is just a system where knowing your rights is optional for
> them and essential for you.

### THE RULE, READ ALOUD (7:00 to 12:00)

Read slowly. Say the citation. Let the pauses work.

Cover, in this order:
1. **You can request an evaluation. In writing.** A verbal ask starts nothing.
   Email counts and creates the paper trail.
2. **The school cannot simply refuse and move on.** If they say no, they owe you
   Prior Written Notice: a written explanation of what they decided, why, what
   information they used, and what other options they considered.
   That is 34 CFR 300.503.
3. **The evaluation clock.** Sixty calendar days, and this is the part that
   trips people: the clock starts when you sign and return the consent form, not
   the day you asked. Some states run 45 days. Check yours. 34 CFR 300.301.
4. **If you disagree with their evaluation**, you can request an Independent
   Educational Evaluation at public expense. The school has exactly two lawful
   responses: pay for it, or file due process to defend theirs. Ignoring you is
   not on the menu. 34 CFR 300.502.

> Read that last one again with me. The school pays. Not you, hoping to get
> reimbursed. That is the rule, and most parents never hear it said out loud.

### WHAT TO SAY (12:00 to 16:00)

Give the exact words. This is the part people replay.

The email:

> I am requesting a full evaluation of my child for special education
> eligibility under IDEA. Please send me the evaluation plan and consent form.
> If the district declines to evaluate, please provide Prior Written Notice
> explaining the decision.

Then:
- Write down the date you sign the consent. Put day 45 in your calendar.
- Ask for the multidisciplinary team, not a single screener.
- If they say no, ask for the refusal in writing. Most "no" answers become "yes"
  at that exact sentence, and that alone tells you something.

> You do not have to be aggressive. You do not have to know the citation by
> heart. You have to be the parent who puts it in writing and knows what day it
> is.

### CLOSE (16:00 to 18:00)

> If you got a "let's wait and see" this week, here is what I want you to take
> from this. You were not being difficult. You were being handed the version of
> the answer that works for a system with too few evaluators and too many kids.
>
> The written answer to this question, with the regulations and the email you
> can copy, is on the site at roycanhelp.org. If you have a question of your own,
> there is a box on the front page. I read all of them.
>
> This is not charity. It is the law. It is owed to your kid.

## Production notes

**Booking guests with this framing.** The capacity argument is what gets state
and federal people on the show. The pitch: your outreach budget is small, my
listeners are the exact families you are trying to reach, and I am not going to
ambush you. Ask them the real question, which is why parents do not hear this
until year three, and let them answer honestly about staffing and funding.

**Legal safety.** Name practices, never districts or individuals. "Districts do
this" is protected and is also the better bit. "Lexington School District Two
does this" is a lawsuit and a smaller point.

**Where episodes come from.** The Ask Roy queue is the show's engine. Each
question is an episode premise that is already in his voice and already
researched. Recording an answer he has already written is the lowest-friction
way to start.

**Cadence beats length.** Twenty minutes every other week, kept up for a year,
beats an hour whenever inspiration strikes. Solo shows die on cadence.
