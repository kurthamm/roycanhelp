ABA, OT, PT, Speech, and the Waitlist That Will Break You

What This Actually Is

Your child needs support. You've heard about ABA, OT, PT, and speech therapy. These are real, evidence-based services, and they actually help. But they're also expensive, often in short supply, and subject to waitlists that can be years long. You'll need to navigate insurance, school, and possibly private therapy all at the same time.

I looked into ABA for my son. The waitlist was 18 months. By the time he would've started, he'd be four. I found private therapy instead. Then I learned Medicaid would have covered it if I'd known to ask. This section is so you don't waste time like I did.

The Main Therapies and What They Actually Do

  • Speech-Language Pathology (SLP): Addresses speech (pronunciation), language (understanding and using words), feeding/swallowing, and voice. If your kid doesn't talk, talks unclearly, or has trouble understanding, speech therapy helps.
  • Physical Therapy (PT): Focuses on gross motor skills—walking, running, jumping, coordination, balance. If your kid's movement lags, PT builds strength and motor planning.
  • Occupational Therapy (OT): Addresses fine motor (pencil grip, buttons, eating utensils), self-care (bathing, toileting, dressing), and sensory processing. OT helps your kid function in daily life.
  • Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA): Intensive behavioral intervention using positive reinforcement to teach skills and reduce challenging behaviors. Often 20–40 hours per week. Very effective for autism, but exhausting and controversial in some autistic communities.
  • Developmental Coaching: Early Intervention version of "behavioral support"—a coach works with you and your child to build skills and address challenges. Less intensive than ABA, fits into family life better.

The first person to tell me my kid needed ABA said "it's evidence-based," then quoted research from 2015 and charged me $300/hour. That's not evidence, that's a sales pitch.

Where Therapy Comes From: School vs. Insurance vs. Private

School (IEP): If your child qualifies for special education, the school provides speech, OT, PT as part of the IEP—at no cost to you. Ask for therapy in the IEP meeting. It's free.

Early Intervention (free): Birth to 3, the state pays. No insurance hassle. Use it.

Insurance: Your job-based or marketplace insurance covers some therapy. Look for visit caps, deductibles, and prior authorization requirements. Your state might mandate coverage for specific therapies (especially ABA for autism). Check your state insurance commissioner's office.

Medicaid: If your child qualifies, Medicaid covers therapy with fewer restrictions than private insurance. It's often more generous than you think.

Private pay: If you're stuck on a waitlist and need therapy now, private therapy exists. It's expensive ($50–400/hour depending on what kind of therapy and your location). But it sometimes gets results faster than waiting for insurance or waiver services.

Getting Insurance to Pay (Or School, Or Medicaid)

  1. Always start with school and Early Intervention. These are free and legal entitlements. Use them first.
  2. For additional therapy, get a prescription from your doctor. Insurance wants a medical reason for therapy. Your pediatrician or developmental pediatrician can provide this. "My child needs speech therapy for speech delay" is enough.
  3. Know your state's insurance mandates. Many states mandate ABA coverage. Some mandate speech/OT/PT for autism or other conditions. Check your state insurance commissioner's website. Link your mandate to your appeal.
  4. Use Medicaid as secondary if you have it. Medicaid picks up what private insurance doesn't cover (copays, deductibles, visits beyond insurance limits).
  5. Appeal denials. Insurance companies deny claims for therapy constantly. Request the clinical justification for the denial. Provide research. Appeal. Most denials get overturned on appeal.

Waitlists and How to Survive Them

Many therapists in your area will have long waitlists. Medicaid waiver services have multi-year waitlists. State-funded programs often have waiting lists. Here's how to navigate it:

  • Get on every list you're eligible for. Early Intervention waitlist, school-based services, Medicaid waiver, private therapy practices—all of them. You can use services from multiple sources.
  • Use free or low-cost services while waiting. Some nonprofits offer low-cost therapy. Your city might have developmental playgroups. Early Intervention (if you're eligible) is free. Use these while waiting for insurance or waiver services.
  • Ask your pediatrician for referrals to providers with shorter waitlists. The provider you want might have an 18-month wait; another might have a 3-month wait. Call around.
  • Ask if your state has telehealth therapy available. Some states expanded telehealth for therapy. This can reduce waitlists because providers can work across state lines (or at least, outside your immediate area).
  • If private therapy is an option, do it while you wait for insurance/waiver to cover. You'll save money later if you can get your kid's skills up now and then transition to insurance-covered therapy.

State Insurance Mandates for ABA (Autism Especially)

Many states mandate insurance coverage for ABA. Requirements vary wildly: some states cover unlimited ABA, others cap it at 30 hours per week or 24 months. Check your state insurance commissioner's office for your state's exact mandate. If your state has an autism insurance mandate and your child has an autism diagnosis, use it. This is your legal right.

Alert: ABA is evidence-based and helps many kids, but it's also controversial in some parts of the autistic community because of past practices and the intensity. Talk to autistic self-advocates, not just clinicians, before committing to ABA long-term.

Down Syndrome and Therapy Needs

Kids with Down syndrome typically benefit from early speech, OT, and PT (muscle tone is usually a factor). Medicaid EPSDT covers these. Get them through Early Intervention while your child is 0–3, then through school if they qualify for special education. If your child has cardiac issues (common with Down syndrome), your cardiologist might recommend specific therapy limitations—follow those.

Autism and Evidence-Based Interventions

The research for autism is strongest for early behavioral intervention (ABA, developmental coaching) and speech/OT/PT. But research isn't destiny—your kid is your kid. ABA works for some kids and not others. Speech therapy helps most kids. OT helps with sensory and self-care. Talk to other parents, read recent research, and don't assume one approach is the only one.

Your Next Step

Make sure your child is enrolled in Early Intervention (birth to 3) or school special education (3+) and ask for therapy in writing. Check your insurance for therapy coverage. Research your state's insurance mandates. Get on your state's Medicaid waiver waiting list if eligible. For state-specific information on therapy access, insurance mandates, and wait times, see By State.
