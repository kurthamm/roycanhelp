Congratulations, the State Now Thinks Your Child Is a Stranger

Two weeks before my son turned 18, the school sent me his transition plan and some paperwork about what changes at 18. I didn't think much of it. Then his birthday came and suddenly the insurance company needed to talk to him, not me. Medicaid had "adult" programs different from the kid programs. The school wasn't his school anymore—they just had to have a plan for what came next. And that "what comes next" was largely my problem to figure out. Nobody handed me a manual for what happens when your 18-year-old adult child needs 24/7 support and can legally refuse it. I had to figure out guardianship, state adult programs, SSI shifts, and what the hell "supported decision-making" even meant. By the time I understood the options, I'd been fumbling for six months.

What This Actually Is

At age 18, your child becomes a legal adult. You lose automatic authority over their medical decisions, educational records, and benefits. The school's transition plan becomes real. SSI and Medicaid shift into adult programs. The whole system changes.

This is where the bureaucracy gets genuinely cruel. For 18 years, you were the decision-maker. At 18, suddenly you're not—unless you get guardianship. And guardianship comes with a lot of power and a lot of responsibility, and it's often not the only option.

Guardianship vs. Supported Decision-Making: Know the Difference

Guardianship: You get full legal authority to make decisions for your adult child. You control healthcare, money, schooling, living situation—everything. Your child loses the legal right to make decisions independently. Guardianship requires court approval and is permanent until modified. Most families need some form of guardianship, but it's also a sledgehammer.

Limited Guardianship: You get authority over some decisions (healthcare, money) but not others (education, living situation). This is smarter than full guardianship in many cases. Ask for it specifically in court.

Supported Decision-Making (SDM): Your child keeps their legal rights and makes decisions with your support. You help them understand options, talk through consequences, and navigate processes—but they decide. This respects their autonomy and is appropriate if your child can understand decisions (even if they need help). Not all states recognize SDM legally, and it's not available if your child can't understand basic decisions.

The real talk: If your child can understand and communicate decisions, talk to a disability attorney about SDM first. If they can't, guardianship is probably necessary. Either way, talk to a lawyer who specializes in this. Don't wing it.

At 18, your kid doesn't become smarter or more independent overnight. But the law says they're an adult, so suddenly the school can't talk to you and the doctor needs permission from your kid to tell you anything. The state basically ghosted you.

SSI and SSDI: The Adult Transition

Your child's SSI gets reevaluated at age 18. Here's what changes:

  • Income limit changes: It's now based on your child's income alone, not family income. There's a federal SSI income limit (which changes every year)—check ssa.gov for the current limit. Above that, benefits reduce by 50 cents per dollar earned.
  • Resource limit changes: Same federal resource limit as before, but it's now their resources, not family resources. Check ssa.gov for current limits.
  • Medical redetermination: SSA will reevaluate whether your child still has a disability. Your child is probably still disabled, but they'll ask. Document the disability. Respond on time.
  • Work incentives exist. Your child can work and keep SSI (partially) and Medicaid. This is called PASS (Plan to Achieve Self-Support). Talk to your local Social Security office about work incentives at age 14-15, not at 18.

Adult Medicaid and Continuing Coverage

Medicaid continues into adulthood, but eligibility changes. Adult Medicaid is usually based on your state's expansion eligibility (which varies by state) or on disability status and income. In most states, kids getting SSI automatically get adult Medicaid. But verify this early—don't assume.

Adult Medicaid often covers fewer therapy services than childhood Medicaid does. Ask about your state's adult waiver programs. Some states have robust adult services; others don't. This is a state-by-state question. Check By State for details.

School Transition Planning (Starting at 14, Really Starting)

Federal law (IDEA) requires transition planning starting at age 14 (or 16 depending on your state). This should be part of your child's transition IEP. It should address:

  • Post-secondary education or vocational training
  • Employment goals and job readiness
  • Independent living skills and support
  • Community participation
  • Coordination with adult services (vocational rehabilitation, Medicaid, SSI)

Make sure the transition plan is real, not just paperwork. Ask what actual steps are being taken toward these goals. If the answer is "nothing yet," push back. Your child needs four years to develop work and life skills before leaving school.

Traps I Fell Into

Not getting guardianship early. At 18, I couldn't access my son's school records or medical information without it. Getting it after age 18 requires proving incompetence in court, which is harder than a proactive guardianship before he turned 18. File paperwork at 17.5.

Not understanding adult SSI redetermination. I thought child SSI just continued. Nope. At 18, they reevaluate using adult disability standards. Make sure you document ongoing disability and respond to every notice from Social Security.

Not planning for Medicaid continuation. My state's adult Medicaid program was worse than child Medicaid. I should have started looking at this at 14, not 18.

Assuming the school would handle transition planning. Schools do transition planning because they're required to. They rarely do it well. You have to push. Ask at every IEP meeting from age 14 on: "What is the actual plan for my child after graduation?" Demand answers.

Down Syndrome and Adulthood

Many adults with Down syndrome can work (with support), live semi-independently, and participate in the community. Your transition plan should include vocational exploration. Adult Medicaid waiver services (if available in your state) can fund supported employment, day programs, and residential support. Start planning at 14, not at 22. Your state's vocational rehabilitation agency is free; use it.

Autism and Adult Services

Adult services for autistic people are inconsistent. Some states have robust adult employment support and community programs. Others don't. Some autistic people work and live independently; others need 24/7 support. Your transition plan should be honest about what your child actually can do, not what you wish they could do. Plan accordingly and use adult services that actually exist in your state.

Your Next Step

At 15: Start actual transition planning in the IEP. Ask for specifics. At 17: Meet with a disability attorney about guardianship, supported decision-making, or both. At 17.5: File guardianship paperwork if needed. At 18: Reevaluate SSI and Medicaid. Coordinate with adult services. For state-specific details on adult services, guardianship laws, and transition planning resources, see By State.
