The Fine Print on SSI Nobody Hands You

SSI letters are short and the deadlines are shorter. Here is what the Social Security regulations actually say about appeals, turning 18, and what counts, with the exact sentences so you can point to them.

A No Is Not the Last Word, and the Clock Is 60 Days

Many families are turned down the first time and never ask for a second look. You can ask. The deadline is 60 days from when you receive the notice, so keep the envelope and write down the date it arrived.

The rule says: “We shall reconsider an initial determination if you or any other party to the reconsideration files a written request at one of our offices within 60 days after the date you receive notice of the initial determination” (20 CFR 416.1409)

Missed it? There is a way back in if you have a good reason.

The rule says: “If you show us that you had good cause for missing the deadline, we will extend the time period” (20 CFR 416.1409)

The next step, a hearing, has the same 60 day window.

The rule says: “The request must be filed at one of our offices within 60 days after the date you receive notice of the previous determination or decision” (20 CFR 416.1433)

Say: “I disagree with this decision and I am requesting reconsideration. Please confirm in writing that you received my request, and the date.”

When Your Child Turns 18, SSI Looks Again

A child who gets SSI is reviewed at 18, under the rules for adults. This surprises families, and it is not a sign that you did anything wrong.

The rule says: “We may find that you are not now disabled even though we previously found that you were disabled” (20 CFR 416.987)

The rule says: “We will redetermine your eligibility either during the 1-year period beginning on your 18th birthday” (20 CFR 416.987)

Social Security has to tell you first, in writing, and the notice has to say you can send in your own evidence and ask for benefits to continue during an appeal.

The rule says: “That you have the right to submit medical and other evidence for our consideration during the redetermination” (20 CFR 416.987)

The rule says: “your right to request continuation of benefits during appeal” (20 CFR 416.987)

Start gathering records before the letter comes. Roy's Ages 14 to 26 page is the place to plan.

Parent Income Stops Counting at 18

If your family was turned down because of what you earn, the rule that counts your income has an end date.

The rule says: “If you are a child living with your parents, we apply the deeming rules to you through the month in which you reach age 18” (20 CFR 416.1165)

So a no at 12 does not have to be a no at 18. The SSI Deeming page explains how it works, including the exception almost nobody hears about.

A Job Can Help More Than It Hurts

Some earnings by a young person in school are set aside before SSI counts anything.

The rule says: “If you are under age 22 and a student who is regularly attending school as described in § 416.1861” (20 CFR 416.1112)

The amounts change every year, so check the current figure with Social Security before you rely on it. Ask about it by name: the student earned income exclusion.

Not Everything Counts as Savings

SSI has a limit on what a person can own, and the home and the car do not count toward it.

The rule says: “An automobile, if used for transportation” (20 CFR 416.1210)

The rule says: “Household goods and personal effects as defined in § 416.1216” (20 CFR 416.1210)

Check the details for your own situation, and see Protecting Benefits before you move any money around.

A Way to Ask That Works

Ask in writing, keep copies, and write down the date you got every notice. Social Security staff are handling a mountain of cases, and a short, clear request is easy to act on. If the answer is still no, the disagree-in-writing guide shows what to do next, and your state's parent center can often point you to free help.
