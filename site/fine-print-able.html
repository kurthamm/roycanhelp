The Fine Print on ABLE Accounts

An ABLE account lets a person with a disability save money without losing benefits the way ordinary savings can. The rules are in federal law, with exact wording you can point to. Here is what the law says, in plain order.

Who Can Have One

An ABLE account is a savings account for a person with a disability that can sit alongside benefits like SSI and Medicaid without counting against them the way ordinary savings would. The law has an age rule, and the number in the law today is 46, not the 26 you may see in older articles.

The law says: “the individual is entitled to benefits based on blindness or disability under title II or XVI of the Social Security Act, and such blindness or disability occurred before the date on which the individual attained age 46” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

You do not have to be on those benefits. There is another way in.

The law says: “a disability certification with respect to such individual is filed with the Secretary for such taxable year” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

That certification can be signed by the individual or by a parent or guardian. Many families set one up for a child. Roy's Protecting Benefits page explains why this matters.

Anyone Can Put Money In

This is the answer for the generous grandparent. The law says a person may contribute, not only the parents.

The law says: “under which a person may make contributions for a taxable year, for the benefit of an individual who is an eligible individual for such taxable year” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

There is a yearly ceiling on what everyone together can put in. It is tied to the federal gift tax exclusion, so it changes from year to year.

The law says: “if such contribution to an ABLE account would result in aggregate contributions from all contributors to the ABLE account for the taxable year exceeding the sum of” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

There is also an extra allowance when the person with the disability puts in money from their own earnings, in cases the law describes.

The law says: “in the case of any contribution by a designated beneficiary described in paragraph (7), the lesser of” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

In that case the extra is the smaller of their earnings or the poverty line for one person. Look up this year's figures, and whether the extra applies to your child, with the ABLE program you are considering.

Say: “What is the contribution limit this year, and how can family members send money without it landing in my child's checking account?”

It Is Set Aside, With Two Catches

Congress said money in an ABLE account is to be ignored when deciding eligibility for programs that look at savings.

The law says: “any amount (including earnings thereon) in the ABLE account” (ABLE Act, Pub. L. 113-295, sec. 103)

The full sentence goes on to say that the account, contributions and spending on qualified expenses are all disregarded. For SSI there are two catches. First, housing spending is treated differently.

The law says: “a distribution for housing expenses (within the meaning of such subsection) shall not be so disregarded” (ABLE Act, Pub. L. 113-295, sec. 103)

Second, there is a top limit for SSI.

The law says: “any amount (including such earnings) in such ABLE account shall be considered a resource of the designated beneficiary to the extent that such amount exceeds $100,000” (ABLE Act, Pub. L. 113-295, sec. 103)

Even then, SSI is paused, not ended, and Medicaid keeps going.

The law says: “shall not be terminated, but shall be suspended, by reason of excess resources of the individual attributable to an amount in the ABLE account” (ABLE Act, Pub. L. 113-295, sec. 103)

Ask your ABLE program or a benefits counselor how housing spending is reported before you use the account for rent.

What You Can Spend It On

The list is broader than people expect, and it is not just medical bills.

The law says: “education, housing, transportation, employment training and support, assistive technology and personal support services, health, prevention and wellness, financial management and administrative services, legal fees, expenses for oversight and monitoring, funeral and burial expenses” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

The test is that the expense is related to the person's disability and for their benefit. Keep receipts.

Two Limits to Know Before You Open One

A person can only have one account.

The law says: “which limits a designated beneficiary to 1 ABLE account for purposes of this section” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

And there is a repayment rule that surprises families. After the person dies, a state may claim money left in the account to repay Medicaid.

The law says: “upon the death of the designated beneficiary, all amounts remaining in the qualified ABLE account not in excess of the amount equal to the total medical assistance paid for the designated beneficiary after the establishment of the account” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

The claim is limited to what Medicaid paid for the person after the account was opened, and it is reduced by Medicaid Buy-In premiums.

The law says: “net of any premiums paid from the account or paid by or on behalf of the beneficiary to a Medicaid Buy-In program” (26 U.S.C. 529A)

Funeral and burial costs are on the spending list above, so planning can matter. A special needs trust works differently, and the right choice depends on your family. Talk with a benefits counselor or an attorney who handles special needs planning, and your state's parent center can often point you to free help.

A Way to Ask That Works

Start with your state's ABLE program. Ask them in writing about fees, the contribution limit, how housing spending is reported to SSI, and whether the account can be opened for a minor by a parent. Keep every statement. The SSI fine print and Turning 18 and 26 pages cover the nearby deadlines.
