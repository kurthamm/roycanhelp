The Meeting Where Eight Professionals and One Box of Tissues Discuss Your Child

My son turned three, and suddenly I was getting letters about "transition planning" and "IEP meetings." Early Intervention vanished overnight like it was never there. One day I had a service coordinator who returned my calls. The next day I had nothing. So I called the school district, and they assigned us an IEP meeting. I walked into that room expecting to talk to a teacher. Instead, I got eight people speaking in acronyms—LRE and FAPE and ESL and IEP and everything else—and I realized I was going to need to learn a completely new language. The IEP is where everything gets written down, though. It's also where I learned that I could actually push back. That was three years into this thing.

What This Actually Is

The IEP (Individualized Education Program) is a legally binding document. The school is required to provide what's written in it. An IEP under IDEA Part B means your child gets special education services. A Section 504 plan means your child gets accommodations but may not be eligible for special education. Most families need an IEP. Some need a 504 Plan. Some families need both.

The IEP is also where every decision about your child's education gets written down. Once it's signed, it's your legal protection. I spent three hours in my first IEP meeting absolutely lost. Nobody was being mean—they were actually trying to help. I just didn't speak the language yet.

IEP vs. Section 504: Know the Difference

An IEP: Your child receives special education services (pull-out instruction, resource room, separate classroom, etc.). The school must provide a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE) in the Least Restrictive Environment (LRE). The school pays for everything. You get an IEP meeting every year (or whenever you request one). Prior written notice is required for changes.

A 504 Plan: Your child receives accommodations only (extra time, modified assignments, preferred seating, modified diet for feeding disorders, etc.). No special education services. Less costly for the school, so sometimes schools push 504 instead of IEP. But if your child needs services, push back. A 504 plan is not enough.

The real talk: If your child needs pull-out services, a separate classroom, or any individualized instruction, you want an IEP. If they can access the regular curriculum with accommodations alone, a 504 might work. But if you're unsure, request special education evaluation. The school is required to evaluate if you ask.

I thought "Least Restrictive Environment" meant my kid could sit with the other kids. Turns out it means a lot of things, most of which involve reading 40 pages of legal jargon to find out what it actually means.

Requesting Evaluation and Getting an IEP Started

  1. Make a written request. Email your school's special education coordinator or principal: "I am requesting a comprehensive special education evaluation for my child." Keep the email. The school has 15 days to respond with an evaluation plan.
  2. You'll get an Evaluation Plan to sign. This outlines what the school will assess (academics, behavior, speech, etc.). You can add anything you think is important. Sign it and return it. Evaluation takes 30–45 days.
  3. The IEP team meets to discuss results. This team includes: you, the school psychologist, the special education teacher, the regular education teacher, a school administrator, related service providers (speech/OT/PT if relevant), and your child (sometimes). They'll present test results and recommend whether your child qualifies for special education.
  4. If your child qualifies, you create the IEP. This document includes: present levels of performance (how your kid is doing now), annual goals (what they'll work toward), services they'll receive, and accommodations they need. Everything goes in writing.
  5. You sign the IEP, and services start. The school is legally required to implement everything on that IEP. If they don't, that's a violation of federal law.

Your Parent Rights in an IEP Meeting

  • You have Prior Written Notice rights. The school must notify you in writing of any changes to your child's evaluation, placement, or IEP. You have 10 days to respond. If you disagree, you can request a meeting.
  • You can request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE). If you disagree with the school's evaluation, you can ask for an IEE at public expense. The school will fight this sometimes, but if you insist, they must provide it.
  • You can bring anyone to the IEP meeting. A parent advocate, a lawyer, your sister, your best friend—anyone. The school can ask you to introduce them, but they can't deny them access.
  • You don't have to sign the IEP if you disagree. You can request to continue the meeting, bring more information, or request mediation or due process. Never sign something you don't agree with.
  • You can request an IEP meeting anytime. You don't have to wait for the annual meeting. If something isn't working, ask for a meeting.

Traps I Fell Into

Signing the IEP at the meeting without reading it. I wanted to be "cooperative." Big mistake. Read every word. Take it home if you need to. Call a parent advocate. The school will have plenty of time.

Not knowing what "LRE" meant. I thought my kid was in mainstream classroom being pulled out for speech. Turns out the school was considering a separate classroom as LRE because of behavior challenges. It's not always what you think it means.

Assuming the school knows best. Teachers are professionals. But they have 20 other kids. You know your kid best. If the school's plan doesn't match what you know about your child, say so. In writing. Always in writing.

Not requesting evaluation early enough. The earlier you have an IEP in place, the earlier your child gets services. Don't wait until your kid is failing. Preventive services exist.

Down Syndrome and School

Down syndrome automatically qualifies your child for special education evaluation. Most kids with Down syndrome will have an IEP because they typically need modified curriculum and related services (speech, OT, PT). Push for as much mainstream classroom time as possible—research shows inclusion benefits kids academically and socially. Use the "Least Restrictive Environment" requirement to your advantage. But also be realistic about what your child can access with support.

Autism and IEPs

Autism qualifies your child for evaluation. Many autistic kids spend most of their day in mainstream classrooms with support. Others need a separate program. There's no one right answer. Look at your state's autism insurance mandates (speech and OT are often covered)—that's different from school services. School pays for services that support education; insurance pays for medical therapy. Both can happen.

When School Won't Evaluate

If you request evaluation and the school says your child doesn't qualify or doesn't need it, get that refusal in writing. Then contact your state's Protection & Advocacy organization or your Parent Training & Information Center (PTI). They provide free advocacy. The school does have to evaluate if you request it; they can't just say no.

Your Next Step

If your child isn't in school yet, start talking to your Early Intervention provider about transition planning at age 2.5. If your child is in school and doesn't have an IEP but you think they should, write the email requesting evaluation today. For advocacy resources and state-specific guidance, see By State.
