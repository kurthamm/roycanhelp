Be in Charge: Understand It Before You Say Yes

At the IEP table or in the exam room, the rule is the same: you are in charge of your child's care. Some days that feels like too much, and nobody should blame a parent for that. So here is the short version: understand it before you say yes, and when you cannot, take one small step.

If You Are Overwhelmed, You Are Not Failing

Every day can be hard. There is paperwork, there are appointments and waiting rooms, and there is never enough time to breathe. Plenty of loving parents take the easy path now and then because there is only so much of one person to go around. That is not a character flaw, and nobody here is keeping score.

You do not have to do all of this at once. Pick the smallest step: one question, one email, one folder. Then rest.

The Rule

Professionals bring training. You bring the child. Nobody knows your child the way you do, and nobody else will live with the result. Being in charge does not mean refusing everything or arguing with everyone. It means you understand what is being proposed, why, and what the other choices are, before you agree to it. This applies to school plans and it applies to medical treatment.

Questions Worth Asking Before You Say Yes

  • What is this for, in plain words?
  • What are the other choices?
  • What are the risks, and what does it cost?
  • What happens if we wait, or say no?
  • How will we know it is working, and when do we check?
  • Can I have that in writing?

If you only have the energy for one, ask the last one: "Can I have that in writing?" It buys you time to think and gives you something to look at later.

Take the questions to school meetings and to doctor visits. For anything medical, your child's doctor is the person to answer them. This page is not medical advice.

At School: Two Separate Yeses

The school must get your informed consent before it does an initial evaluation of your child, and consent for the evaluation is not consent for special education services (34 CFR 300.300). Those are two decisions, and you get to make each one on its own.

When the school proposes or refuses to change your child's evaluation, services, or placement, it has to tell you in writing what it plans and explain why (34 CFR 300.503). Read it. Ask about anything that is vague. Roy's Wisdom page has a lesson about agreeing to everything the school said without asking for numbers. Ask for numbers: how many minutes, how often, who provides it, and how progress will be measured.

Keep Your Own Records

School records. You have the right to inspect and review your child's education records. The school has to comply within a reasonable time, and not more than 45 days after it receives your request, and it has to respond to reasonable requests for explanations and interpretations of the records (34 CFR 99.10).

Medical records. Under the federal health privacy rule, a person has a right to inspect and get a copy of their health information, and the provider must act on the request within 30 days (45 CFR 164.524). Where a parent has authority to make health care decisions for a minor child, the rule generally treats the parent as the child's personal representative (45 CFR 164.502). There are exceptions, and state laws vary, so ask the office how it handles requests.

A one-page summary of your child's conditions, medicines, doctors and insurance saves you from rewriting the same history again and again. Roy's Wisdom page has a lesson on keeping it current.

If You Do Not Agree

You are allowed to say so. Say that you disagree, say why, and ask for the answer in writing. The disagree-in-writing guide shows what comes next at school, with Early Intervention, and with Medicaid.

And you do not have to do it alone. Every state has a Parent Training and Information Center that helps families for free: find yours here.

A Calm Way to Say It

"Thank you. I want to understand this before I agree to it. Could you walk me through it and send it to me in writing?"

That is firm, polite, and hard to argue with.

If something is not urgent, "I would like some time to think about this" is a perfectly good sentence, and a reasonable one to say out loud.
