The Fine Print Nobody Hands You in Early Intervention (Birth to Three)

Early Intervention moves fast and the paperwork moves faster. Here are real rules from the federal regulations that work in your child's favor, each with the exact sentence so you can point to it, and something friendly to say.

You Can Make the Call Yourself

You do not need a doctor to start this, and you do not need to wait for someone to tell you it is time. The rules list parents among the people who can refer a child.

The rule says: “Parents, including parents of infants and toddlers” (34 CFR 303.303)

Roy's Birth to Three page covers how to find the number in your state.

The 45 Day Clock

Once the program has your referral, there is a deadline. The first evaluation, the first assessments, and the first meeting to write the plan are supposed to be finished in 45 days.

The rule says: “must be completed within 45 days from the date the lead agency or EIS provider receives the referral of the child” (34 CFR 303.310)

There are exceptions, like a family emergency that is written down in the child's record, or a parent who has not given consent after repeated documented tries. So write down the date you called and who you spoke to. If the weeks start slipping, ask what the date is and what is holding it up.

Say: “I made the referral on this date. Can you tell me the date the 45 days are up, and when the evaluation and the first plan meeting are scheduled?”

Some Things Cost You Nothing, Ever

Whatever your state does about fees for services, a short list of things cannot be charged to parents at all.

The rule says: “The following are required functions that must be carried out at public expense, and for which no fees may be charged to parents” (34 CFR 303.521)

The list includes evaluation and assessment, service coordination, and the work of putting together and reviewing the plan. If a bill or a form suggests otherwise, ask where in your state's written payment policy it says so.

Not Being Able to Pay Cannot Slow Things Down

Some states charge fees for some services, on a sliding scale. If yours does, the rule has a promise built in.

The rule says: “The inability of the parents of an infant or toddler with a disability to pay for services will not result in a delay or denial of services under this part to the child or the child's family” (34 CFR 303.521)

If money is the worry, say so out loud and ask for your state's written policy on payments and what happens if you cannot pay.

The Move at Age Three Starts Early

Early Intervention usually ends at three, and school services work differently. The rules do not leave the handoff to chance. The program has to tell the school district well before the birthday.

The rule says: “not fewer than 90 days before the third birthday of the toddler with a disability if that toddler may be eligible for preschool services under part B of the Act, the lead agency notifies the SEA and the LEA for the area in which the toddler resides” (34 CFR 303.209)

It also has to set up a meeting with you and the school district, if you approve.

The rule says: “with the approval of the family of the toddler, convenes a conference, among the lead agency, the family, and the LEA” (34 CFR 303.209)

That meeting is to be held at least 90 days before the third birthday, and with everyone's agreement it can be as early as 9 months before. Ask for it early. The School Years page picks up from there.

One more thing to ask about. Some states let families keep Early Intervention going past the third birthday instead of moving straight to school services. It is a state choice, so it depends on where you live.

The rule says: “may choose the continuation of early intervention services under this part for his or her child after the child turns three until the child enters, or is eligible under State law to enter, kindergarten or elementary school” (34 CFR 303.211)

Say: “My child turns three on this date. When is the transition meeting with the school district, and who is coordinating it? Does our state let us continue Early Intervention past three?”

You Can See Everything in the File

The early intervention record on your child and your family is yours to look at.

The rule says: “afforded the opportunity to inspect and review all part C early intervention records about the child and the child's family that are collected, maintained, or used under this part” (34 CFR 303.401)

Say: “I would like to review my child's early intervention records, including the evaluations and the plan. How do I get them?”

A Way to Ask That Works

Short, friendly, in writing. What you are asking for, the date, and a thank you. Most people at the other end are carrying more cases than they should, and a clear request makes their job easier. If the answer is still no, the disagree-in-writing guide shows the next steps.
