So You Have a Diagnosis. The Casserole Brigade Is On Its Way.

We got the call on a Tuesday: "We have some concerns about your son's development." I spent the rest of that day pretending I was fine while my hands shook. By Thursday, I was sitting in a pediatrician's office hearing the word "autism," and I honestly can't remember what I said after that. I just remember the paperwork—so much paperwork. And then people started showing up with casseroles, which was kind, except what I needed wasn't food. I needed someone to tell me what came next.

What This Actually Is

The diagnosis is real. Your kid is real. The grief is real too, and it's okay to feel it. You're mourning the life you thought you'd have, and that's not weakness—it's love. You're going to cry, and then you're going to schedule appointments, and both of those things are normal.

Here's what they don't tell you in the pediatrician's office: a diagnosis isn't the end of information. It's the beginning. And there's actually a roadmap, buried under a lot of jargon and paperwork, but it's there. After my son was diagnosed with autism, I felt like I was learning a new language. I was. It's bureaucratic, it's stupidly acronym-heavy, and once you learn it, you realize you're already fluent in grief. Everything else is just paperwork.

What To Do First (The Next 90 Days)

  1. Find your state's Early Intervention program (birth to age 3). Call your state health department's developmental disabilities hotline. In most states, this is free. Search for "[Your State] early intervention" or visit ectacenter.org to find your state coordinator. Your kid qualifies if there's a developmental delay or disability risk. You don't need a referral; you can call directly.
  2. Request a comprehensive evaluation. Early Intervention is required by federal law (IDEA Part C) to evaluate your child at no cost. Write an email requesting evaluation. Keep copies. Don't wait for anyone to call you back.
  3. Get it in writing. Ask for everything in writing—the evaluation plan, the results, the timeline, the IFSP (Individualized Family Service Plan). If it's not written down, it didn't happen. I learned this the hard way.
  4. Understand your diagnostic paperwork. Read the evaluation report. Highlight the specific delays or areas of concern. You don't need to understand every test score, but you need to know what your kid actually struggles with versus what the bureaucracy thinks they struggle with.
  5. Talk to other parents. Find a parent support group through your state's disability agency or through Parent Training and Information Centers (PTIs). Start here: parentcenterhub.org. Other parents have been in the waiting room. They know things.
  6. Get health insurance clear. Call your insurance company and ask what your coverage is for evaluations, therapies, and specialist visits. Ask for it in writing. Ask if there are age limits, prior authorization requirements, or state-specific mandates. Write it down.

In the first week after diagnosis, I got three casseroles I couldn't eat and zero answers to the questions I actually needed answered. The casseroles were delicious, but I wanted someone to just tell me what Part C was.

Traps I Fell Into

Waiting for the school. I thought special education started in school. Wrong. If your kid is under 3, you have Early Intervention. If you have a suspicion of disability before school age, act on it now. Waiting costs you access to free services and time.

Believing the first doctor who said "wait and see." Some delays catch up on their own. Some don't. If a developmental specialist has concerns, get a second opinion, but don't sit still waiting for a problem to fix itself. You have federal law on your side to get free evaluation.

Not keeping copies of everything. Every test report, every email, every doctor's note. Scan them, back them up. You'll need them for the IEP meeting in preschool. You'll need them for SSI paperwork. You'll need them for insurance appeals. Start a folder now.

Assuming the diagnosis explains everything. A label is useful for getting services, but it's not your kid. Your kid is still your kid. The diagnosis tells you where to look for help. It doesn't tell you who they are.

Down Syndrome? Here's What's Different

Down syndrome is a genetic condition, not something that develops—your child was born with it. Evaluation works the same way (Early Intervention for 0–3, free assessment), but you'll hear a lot of medical information: heart screenings, thyroid checks, hearing tests. These aren't optional. They're part of the early support plan. Medicaid will cover most of this if your family qualifies, and most families with a Down syndrome diagnosis do. We'll talk about that in the "Paying for Care" section.

Autism Specifically

Autism can be diagnosed as early as 18 months, but many kids aren't diagnosed until kindergarten or later. If you have early concerns—language delay, social differences, sensory sensitivities—trust your gut. Early Intervention is still free and still available. Some states have autism-specific insurance mandates (we'll cover this in "Therapies"). Many states mandate coverage for ABA (Applied Behavior Analysis). Check yours.

Your Next Step

Take this one state at a time. Your state has resources—sometimes buried in bad websites with 1997 design, but they're there. Head to By State to find your Early Intervention program's contact info and the disability resource coordinator for your state.

You don't have to understand everything today. You don't have to feel okay about this. But you do have to make the phone call. So make it tomorrow.
