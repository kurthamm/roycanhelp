The Fine Print on Turning 18 and Turning 26

The years from 14 to 26 are full of dates that nobody puts on a calendar for you. Here are the rules behind three of them, with the exact sentences so you can point to them, and what to say.

At 16, the Plan Is About Life After School

No later than the first IEP in effect when your child turns 16 (or younger, if the team agrees), the plan has to look past graduation.

The rule says: “Beginning not later than the first IEP to be in effect when the child turns 16, or younger if determined appropriate by the IEP Team, and updated annually, thereafter, the IEP must include” (34 CFR 300.320)

That means goals for training, education, employment and, where it fits, independent living, plus the services and courses needed to get there. Your child gets a seat at that meeting.

The rule says: “the public agency must invite a child with a disability to attend the child's IEP Team meeting if a purpose of the meeting will be the consideration of the postsecondary goals for the child” (34 CFR 300.321)

With your consent (or your child's, once they reach the age of majority), and to the extent appropriate, the school must also invite agencies that may help provide or pay for services after school.

The rule says: “the public agency must invite a representative of any participating agency that is likely to be responsible for providing or paying for transition services” (34 CFR 300.321)

Say: “What are the postsecondary goals in the plan, and which agencies are invited to the next meeting? Can we invite vocational rehabilitation or the adult services agency?”

The Year Before the Age of Majority

In many states, a young person's school rights move from the parents to the young person when they reach the age of majority. Nobody should find that out on the birthday. The school has to tell the student ahead of time.

The rule says: “Beginning not later than one year before the child reaches the age of majority under State law, the IEP must include a statement that the child has been informed of the child's rights under Part B of the Act, if any, that will transfer to the child on reaching the age of majority” (34 CFR 300.320)

Whether rights transfer at all is up to each state, so ask what yours does.

The rule says: “A State may provide that, when a child with a disability reaches the age of majority under State law that applies to all children” (34 CFR 300.520)

The rule makes an exception for a child who has been found to be incompetent under state law.

The rule says: “except for a child with a disability who has been determined to be incompetent under State law” (34 CFR 300.520)

When a state does transfer rights, the school has to send notices to both the young person and the parents. And if the young person cannot give informed consent, the state must have a way to appoint a parent or another appropriate person to represent their educational interests.

The rule says: “The public agency must provide any notice required by this part to both the child and the parents” (34 CFR 300.520)

Say: “What is the age of majority in our state, what happens to my child's school rights then, and what is the process if my child needs help making decisions? Who can we talk to about the options?”

Guardianship is a big decision with other paths around it. Your state's parent center can walk you through what exists where you live, and it is free.

Staying on a Parent's Health Plan to 26

This is one of the best-known rules and still one of the most missed deadlines. If a plan offers coverage for dependent children, it has to keep offering it until the child reaches 26.

The rule says: “must make such coverage available for children until attainment of 26 years of age” (45 CFR 147.120)

And the plan cannot make it harder for an adult child by looking at their situation.

The rule says: “a plan or issuer may not deny or restrict dependent coverage for a child who has not attained age 26 based on the presence or absence of the child's financial dependency” (45 CFR 147.120)

The rule also leaves a door open for special cases.

The rule says: “Other requirements of Federal or State law, including section 609 of ERISA or section 1908 of the Social Security Act, may require coverage of certain children” (45 CFR 147.120)

The federal rule itself stops at 26, but as that sentence says, other laws may require coverage for some children. So ask the plan in writing, well before the birthday, whether it continues coverage for an adult child with a disability, what proof it needs, and by what date. Your state insurance department or parent center can tell you about state rules. Roy's Ages 14 to 26 page is where the 26 warning comes from.

Say: “My child turns 26 on this date. Does this plan continue coverage for an adult child with a disability, what documents do you need, and what is the deadline to send them?”

Money at 18

Turning 18 changes SSI and Medicaid too. The SSI fine print covers the review at 18 and when parent income stops counting, and the Medicaid fine print covers hearings and notices if a no comes in the mail.

A Way to Ask That Works

Put dates on your calendar for 16, one year before the age of majority, 18, and a full year before 26. Ask early, in writing, and thank people. Most of the people you will talk to want to help and are working with more cases than time. If the answer is no, the disagree-in-writing guide shows what comes next.
