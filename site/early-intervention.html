Birth to Three: When the State Pays for Therapy (And Actually Means It)

Early in our journey, someone mentioned "Early Intervention" and I had no idea what they meant. I thought I had to wait for school. I thought we had to pay for therapy. Turns out, if your kid is under three and has a disability, your state has to provide services for free. Legitimately free. No copays, no deductibles, no "sorry our insurance won't cover that." I didn't call right away. That was a mistake. The years from birth to three are the only time the government actually acts like they're on your side. Don't wait like I did.

What This Actually Is

Early Intervention (Part C) is free. I mean actually, truly free. No insurance required. Developmental services for kids birth to age three with disabilities or developmental delays. It's mandated by federal law (IDEA Part C). Every state has it. The problem is almost nobody knows about it until it's too late.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the state will pay for speech therapy, physical therapy, occupational therapy, developmental coaching, and family training during these three years. No prior authorization. No insurance denials. No "we'll cover 50% after your deductible." Free. The state offers this because early intervention works, and it saves them money later. So they actually try to fund it.

How to Get In (Do This Now)

  1. Call your state's Early Intervention coordinator. This is your one-stop shop. Search "[Your State] Early Intervention" or find your state's contact at ectacenter.org. Your pediatrician can refer you, but you don't need them to. You can call directly and request an evaluation.
  2. Request an evaluation in writing (email is fine). Say your child has concerns (developmental delay, speech delay, hearing loss, whatever applies). The state has to evaluate within 45 days. Faster if it's an urgent concern. Save the email. They'll respond with your Evaluation Plan.
  3. The evaluation happens at no cost. A multidisciplinary team (speech, PT, OT, developmental psychologist) assesses your child. They'll ask a lot of questions about development, health history, family background. Bring any medical records you have. This takes a few hours, usually broken into two to three visits.
  4. If your child qualifies, you get an IFSP (Individualized Family Service Plan). This is different from an IEP (we'll cover that in "School & IEPs"). The IFSP is focused on your whole family, not just your kid. They'll ask about your family's priorities and concerns. The IFSP outlines which services your kid gets, how often, and where (usually in your home).
  5. Services start immediately. Once you sign the IFSP, therapy begins. Most families get 1-3 sessions per week depending on their kid's needs and the IFSP. Your service coordinator stays assigned to your case and coordinates everything. You don't bounce between specialists.

Early Intervention is the only part of special education that feels like the government is actually on your side. Fair warning: it ends when your kid turns three, and then they throw you back into the school system like you're not actually their problem anymore.

What Services Look Like

  • Speech-Language Pathology (SLP): For feeding, speech, language, and swallowing. If your kid isn't talking, has trouble eating, or doesn't respond to sounds, this is your service.
  • Physical Therapy (PT): For gross motor skills: sitting, crawling, walking, coordination. If your kid is slow to meet these milestones, PT helps.
  • Occupational Therapy (OT): For fine motor skills, self-care, sensory processing. Feeding challenges often get OT, too.
  • Special Instruction: Developmental coaching focused on learning, play skills, and cognitive development.
  • Family Training and Counseling: The therapist teaches you strategies to use between sessions. This is actually the most important part. You're your kid's therapist 24/7; the professional is there two hours a week.

Traps I Fell Into

Waiting until diagnosis to call. Early Intervention doesn't require a diagnosis. A "developmental concern" is enough to request evaluation. I waited six months for a diagnosis when I could have had a therapist working with my son the whole time.

Thinking services are bad because they're free. They're not. Early Intervention therapists are licensed professionals. The IFSP is a legal document. Your state is required to provide services that will help. Some services are better than others (that part is universal), but the system itself is solid.

Not using the service coordinator as your advocate. They're assigned to you. Use them. They know the system, they know the state requirements, and they work for you, not the system. Bring your list of questions to every meeting.

Thinking "this is temporary, they'll catch up." Maybe they will. But if there's a developmental concern, why not use these three years when services are free and easy to access? You can always stop services. You can't get those years back.

Down Syndrome in Early Intervention

Kids with Down syndrome automatically qualify for Early Intervention. You don't need to wait for a delay to show up; the diagnosis itself is enough. Call immediately after birth if you know your child has Down syndrome. You'll want speech, PT, and OT starting early. These years are critical for muscle tone, feeding, and language development. Medicaid will cover services during Early Intervention regardless of family income (once your Medicaid application is approved, which we'll cover in "Paying for Care").

Autism in Early Intervention

Autism diagnosis qualifies your child for services. Some states will start evaluation based on parental concern alone (speech delay, social differences) without waiting for an autism diagnosis. If you suspect autism, call. The evaluation team includes developmental psychology expertise in most states, and they can pick up autism early. Early intervention doesn't "fix" autism, but it builds language, motor skills, and social foundations that make everything else easier.

What Happens at Three

Services stop. Early Intervention ends. Unless your child qualifies for school-based special education (which we'll cover in "School & IEPs"), you lose your service coordinator and your free therapy. Some states have bridge programs or transition planning, but most families hit a cliff. Start talking to your Early Intervention coordinator about transition planning at age 2.5. Your state should have preschool special education programs if your child still qualifies.

Your Next Step

Find your state Early Intervention program at ectacenter.org, call this week, and ask to request an evaluation. Don't worry about whether your child "really" qualifies. Let the multidisciplinary team make that call. That's literally their job.

For state-specific details on how to access Early Intervention in your area, check By State.
