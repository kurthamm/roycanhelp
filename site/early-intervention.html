Birth to Three: When the State Pays for Therapy (And Actually Means It)

Early in our journey, someone mentioned "Early Intervention" and I had no idea what they meant. I thought I had to wait for school. I thought we had to pay for therapy. Turns out, if your kid is under three and has a disability, your state has to provide services for free. Legitimately free. No copays, no deductibles, no "sorry our insurance won't cover that." I didn't call right away. That was a mistake. The years from birth to three are the only time the government actually acts like they're on your side. Don't wait like I did.

What This Actually Is

Early Intervention (Part C) is free. I mean actually, truly free. No insurance required. Developmental services for kids birth to age three with disabilities or developmental delays. It's mandated by federal law (IDEA Part C, 34 CFR Part 303). Every state has it. The problem is almost nobody knows about it until it's too late.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the state will pay for speech therapy, physical therapy, occupational therapy, developmental coaching, and family training during these three years. No prior authorization. No insurance denials. No "we'll cover 50% after your deductible." Free. The state offers this because early intervention works, and it saves them money later. So they actually try to fund it. Your child gets a service coordinator assigned to your family to make sure nothing falls through the cracks.

How to Get In (Do This Now)

  1. Call your state's Early Intervention coordinator. No doctor needed. Anybody can refer a child to Early Intervention. That includes you. You don't need a pediatrician's permission. You don't need a diagnosis. You just need to suspect a developmental concern (34 CFR 303.302-303). Find your state's contact at ectacenter.org or search "[Your State] Early Intervention." When you call, ask to request an evaluation. That's it.
  2. Request an evaluation in writing (email works). The state has 45 days from the date they receive your referral to complete the evaluation, do initial assessments, hold an IFSP meeting, and have a signed plan in place (34 CFR 303.310). That 45-day clock starts when they get your referral. Write down the date you send the email. If the state drags, you can reference that timeline.
  3. The evaluation happens at no cost. A multidisciplinary team (speech, PT, OT, developmental specialist) assesses your child. They'll ask a lot of questions about development, health history, family background, and your family's priorities. Bring any medical records you have. This usually takes a few hours, spread across two or three visits.
  4. If your child qualifies, you get an IFSP (Individualized Family Service Plan). This is different from an IEP (we'll cover that in "School & IEPs"). The IFSP must identify your family's concerns and priorities, outline which services your kid gets, where services happen (usually your home), and how often (34 CFR 303.340). Services are provided in natural environments unless the team decides otherwise. You are a full partner in this meeting, not a spectator.
  5. Services start within the 45-day window. Once you sign the IFSP, therapy can begin immediately. Some states will even start services before the evaluation is finished (an "interim IFSP") if the need is urgent and you consent (34 CFR 303.345). Most families get 1-3 sessions per week. Your service coordinator stays assigned to your case and coordinates all the pieces. You don't bounce between different people.

Early Intervention is the only part of special education that feels like the government is actually on your side. Fair warning: it ends when your kid turns three, and then they throw you back into the school system like you're not actually their problem anymore.

What Services Look Like

Part C covers these services (34 CFR 303.13):

  • Speech-Language Pathology (SLP): For feeding, speech, language, and swallowing. If your kid isn't talking, has trouble eating, or doesn't respond to sounds, this is your service.
  • Physical Therapy (PT): For gross motor skills: sitting, crawling, walking, coordination. If your kid is slow to meet these milestones, PT helps.
  • Occupational Therapy (OT): For fine motor skills, self-care, sensory processing. Feeding challenges often get OT, too.
  • Special Instruction: Developmental coaching focused on learning, play skills, and cognitive development.
  • Family Training and Counseling: The therapist teaches you strategies to use between sessions. This is actually the most important part. You're your kid's therapist 24/7. The professional is there a few hours a week. You need strategies that work in real life.
  • Service Coordination: Your assigned service coordinator handles scheduling, paperwork, insurance questions, and system navigation. They work for you, not the government.

Traps I Fell Into

Waiting until diagnosis to call. Early Intervention doesn't require a diagnosis. A developmental concern is enough. You can request evaluation based on a speech delay, motor delay, hearing concerns, or just "something feels off" to you. Don't wait for a pediatrician to agree or a diagnosis to arrive. Call now.

Thinking services are bad because they're free. They're not. Early Intervention therapists are licensed professionals. The IFSP is a legal document with federal requirements (34 CFR 303.340-345). Your state is legally required to provide services. Some services are better than others, but the system itself is solid and properly funded.

Not understanding the fee issue. Evaluations are 100% free everywhere. Services are free everywhere. States may charge a sliding-scale fee based on family income, but inability to pay cannot be a reason to deny services (34 CFR 303.520-521). If a state asks for fees, ask about their sliding scale and their waiver policy. Don't assume you have to pay.

Not using the service coordinator as your advocate. They're assigned to you. Use them. They know the system, they know the state requirements, and they work for you. Bring your questions to every meeting. If you disagree with a recommendation, your service coordinator has to help you understand your options and your rights.

Thinking "this is temporary, they'll catch up." Maybe they will. But these years from birth to three are the only time the government actively funds therapy at zero cost. After three, services stop unless your child qualifies for school-based special education. Why not use these free years to build strong foundations? You can stop services anytime. You can't get those years back.

Down Syndrome in Early Intervention

Kids with Down syndrome automatically qualify for Early Intervention. You don't need to wait for a delay to show up. The diagnosis itself is enough (34 CFR 303.301-302). Call immediately after birth or diagnosis. You'll want speech, PT, and OT starting early. These years are critical for muscle tone, feeding, and language development. No evaluation is needed; the diagnosis alone makes your child eligible. Get your service coordinator assigned right away so you don't waste time waiting. If your family has Medicaid, coverage will include services during Early Intervention regardless of family income (once your application is approved, which we'll cover in "Paying for Care").

Autism in Early Intervention

Autism diagnosis qualifies your child for services immediately. But don't wait for a diagnosis. Early Intervention will evaluate based on developmental concern alone: speech delay, limited eye contact, repetitive behaviors, social differences. Some states will start services even while evaluation is pending if clinical opinion suggests risk of autism (34 CFR 303.320-321). Call as soon as you notice differences. The evaluation team includes developmental specialists who can pick up early signs of autism. Early intervention doesn't "cure" autism, but it builds language, motor skills, social understanding, and communication patterns that make the preschool years much easier.

What Happens at Three: The Transition Trap

This is the cliff. When your child turns three, Early Intervention services stop. Federal law requires that preschool special education (Part B) take over if your child still qualifies, but there are important differences (34 CFR 303.209). Early Intervention is family-centered and home-based. Preschool is school-centered. Not all kids who qualify for Part C will qualify for Part B. Not all states have robust preschool programs. You could lose services entirely.

At least 90 days before your child's third birthday, your Early Intervention team must hold a transition conference with you and the school district (34 CFR 303.209(b)). At this meeting, they'll discuss whether your child qualifies for Part B, what services might look like, and your options. Some states offer continued Early Intervention services under Part C for a few months after age 3 if the school isn't ready. Some don't. Know your state's rules. Start asking your service coordinator at age 2.5 what transition looks like in your state. Don't be surprised on your child's third birthday.

Your Next Step

Find your state Early Intervention program at ectacenter.org or search "[Your State] Early Intervention." Call this week. You don't need to ask permission. You don't need a doctor's note. You don't need a diagnosis. You just need to say, "I'd like to request an evaluation." Write down the date and person you spoke to. The 45-day clock starts when the program receives your referral, so document it. Don't worry about whether your child "really" qualifies. That's the evaluation team's job. Your job is to call.

For state-specific details on how to access Early Intervention in your area, check By State.

What to say when you call: "I'm requesting an evaluation for my child under Early Intervention Part C. My child is [age] and I have concerns about [speech / hearing / motor skills / development / whatever applies]. Can you send me information about the referral process and what to expect?"
