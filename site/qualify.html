Does My Child Qualify? (Probably For More Than You Think)

The Quick Answer

Your child probably qualifies for more benefits than you realize. Most kids with disabilities qualify for at least three or four programs. The trick is knowing which ones and how to apply. Here's a breakdown:

Program What It Is Who Actually Qualifies Where to Apply
IDEA Part C (Early Intervention) Free developmental services (speech, OT, PT, developmental coaching) for kids 0–3. Paid by the state. Option A: Diagnosed disability (cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, autism, hearing loss, etc.) with high probability of developmental delay (34 CFR 303.21). Option B: Developmental delay measured in one or more areas: cognitive development, physical development (including vision and hearing), communication, social/emotional development, or adaptive development. Each state sets the delay threshold (often 25–33% delay in one area). No IEP yet, just need evaluation and eligibility finding. State Early Intervention coordinator or state health department. Call and request evaluation. You don't need a referral.
IDEA Part B (School Special Education) Free special education services in school (resource room, self-contained classroom, inclusion support, therapies). Ages 3–21. Two-part test (both required): (1) Child has one of 13 federal disability categories (autism, deaf-blindness, deafness, emotional disturbance, hearing impairment, intellectual disability, multiple disabilities, orthopedic impairment, other health impairment, specific learning disability, speech/language impairment, traumatic brain injury, or visual impairment). (2) By reason of disability, child needs special education. School evaluates both. Having the disability alone is not enough; school must find it adversely affects education and special instruction is required. Your local school district special education coordinator. Request evaluation in writing. See School & IEPs for detail on the request letter.
Section 504 Plan Accommodations (extra time on tests, modified assignments, accessible seating, medical procedures like feeding tube monitoring or medication administration) without special education. Any child whose physical or mental impairment substantially limits a major life activity. Major life activities include: learning, self-care, hearing, seeing, speaking, breathing, working, concentrating, and performing manual tasks (34 CFR 104.3). Does not require IDEA disability category. Example: a child with anxiety that limits learning could qualify even if not IDEA-eligible. School district 504 coordinator or special education director. Request evaluation in writing. Not as formal as IDEA but legally binding under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act.
SSI (Supplemental Security Income) Monthly cash payment from Social Security. Amount varies by state and changes annually. Child disability: SSA evaluates based on marked and severe functional limitations across five areas: motor function, cognitive/communicative function, social function, personal function, and concentration/persistence/pace (SSA Blue Book 112.00 Mental Disorders Childhood, but applies to all conditions). Evaluated separately from IDEA. IDEA eligibility helps but is not automatic SSI approval. Income and resources: Family income and resources below federal limits (check ssa.gov for current limits; typically very low-income only). Special note: Down syndrome (Trisomy 21, non-mosaic) is on the Compassionate Allowances list, which flags applications for expedited disability determination. See ssa.gov's Compassionate Allowances page for details on how the expedited process works. ssa.gov or local Social Security office. Bring diagnosis letter from doctor, medical records, recent test results, IEP if available. You don't have to wait for "perfect" documentation; apply now.
Medicaid (Child) Health insurance covering doctor visits, therapy, hospitalization, prescription drugs, and preventive care including EPSDT. Standard Medicaid: Low-income family (varies by state; check your state's Medicaid office for current limits). Expanded Medicaid for kids with disabilities: Many states have separate pathways for children with disabilities regardless of family income, or with much higher income limits. Do not assume you don't qualify. Many families surprise themselves by qualifying. Your state's Medicaid office (often combined with welfare/TANF office) or online application. Call your state Medicaid hotline and ask if you qualify for child disability coverage. Bring proof of income, birth certificate, and disability documentation (IEP or letter from doctor).
Medicaid Waiver (HCBS) Medicaid-funded long-term services at home or community: therapy, respite care, supported employment, residential support. Most states have waiting lists (often several years). Income eligibility based on child's own income only (not family income, even though you think your family income is too high): Child's income typically must not exceed SSI Federal Benefit Rate or 300% of FBR depending on program (check your state's Medicaid office). Institutional deeming rules apply; parents' income doesn't count. Medical/functional requirement: Child must have disability requiring long-term support. Specific requirements and waiting list status vary by state and program. Your state's Medicaid office or disability services agency. This is critical: get on the waiting list immediately if your child might qualify, even if you don't need services now. Many lists are years long; you can use services later.
CHIP (Children's Health Insurance Program) Health insurance for kids in families between Medicaid income limit and marketplace affordability. Premiums capped at 5% of household income, often free or low-cost. Family income above your state's Medicaid limit but below CHIP limit. CHIP income limits typically 200–400% of federal poverty level (varies by state; in 2026, 200% FPL is roughly 32K for a family of 4). No disability requirement; any child qualifies if income fits. No waiting period under continuous eligibility mandate. InsureKidsNow.gov (searchable by state) or your state's CHIP program office. Or apply through healthcare.gov. No referral needed; direct enrollment.
ABLE Account Tax-advantaged savings account for people with disabilities. Does not count against SSI or Medicaid resource limits. See Protecting the Money for full details. Any person with a disability who is SSI-eligible, receiving SSI, or receiving disability benefits. No income or resource limit to open. Available as of 2026 for people with onset by age 46. ablenrc.org. Can open online in minutes.
Medicaid EPSDT Preventive health screenings, diagnosis, and treatment for kids on Medicaid. Covers dental, vision, hearing, developmental screening, therapy, and much more. Often more comprehensive than standard insurance. Automatic for any child on Medicaid under age 21. No separate application. Does not require special education eligibility or disability finding. Ask your Medicaid office or pediatrician what EPSDT services are available in your state. Ask specifically about therapy coverage and developmental screening. Many families don't know EPSDT exists and don't access these services.

The Apply Anyway Principle

Here is something nobody tells you: eligibility rules are misunderstood even by the people enforcing them.

  • A screener's "you won't qualify" is not a determination. When you call or go in-person, staff members often make snap judgments based on what they heard wrong or assumptions they make. This is NOT an official decision. Only a formal written determination counts.
  • Submit the application anyway. If a screener says "you probably don't qualify," ask for the application form anyway. Take it home. Fill it out. Submit it. The agency has to evaluate the written application formally, following federal rules. Your circumstance on paper may be different from what the screener understood verbally.
  • Denials are appealable. If the agency denies your application, you have appeal rights. For Medicaid and CHIP, you get a formal appeal hearing. For IDEA, you get a due process hearing. For SSI, you get a reconsideration. These appeals are free. You can represent yourself or get a lawyer. Many denials are overturned on appeal.
  • Income and resources are calculated differently for different programs. Your family income might be too high for SSI but not for Medicaid. Your child's own income might qualify for a Medicaid waiver even if your family income is high. Just because one program says "no" does not mean another will. Apply to each separately.
  • Your child's disability documentation improves with time. If you apply to SSI now without complete medical records, the agency will request them. If you have an IEP coming or a specialist appointment next month, you can still apply now and supplement later during the evaluation process.

How to Apply for Multiple Programs

  1. Start with Early Intervention (if your child is 0–3). Call your state EI coordinator. It's free and takes just a phone call to request evaluation.
  2. Apply for Medicaid immediately. Even if you think you don't qualify, apply. Many families qualify for expanded Medicaid for kids with disabilities. Your state's website has the application, or call your Medicaid office.
  3. Get on the Medicaid waiver waiting list if available. Most states have waitlists, and some are years long. Get on the list now; you can use services later if you need them.
  4. Apply for SSI if your family income qualifies. Go to ssa.gov or your local Social Security office. Bring diagnosis documentation.
  5. Request school evaluation at age 3 (or earlier if you have concerns). Write your school district special education coordinator and request evaluation. Early special education is worth the effort.
  6. Open an ABLE account if your child has a disability and qualifies for SSI. You don't have to put money in it immediately, but having it open gives you a tool for saving without losing benefits.

You can qualify for so many programs that understanding them all requires a law degree and a spreadsheet. I made a spreadsheet. Then I lost it. Then I made another one.

Important Notes on Program Interactions

  • You don't have to choose between programs. Most families use Early Intervention AND school special education AND Medicaid (for therapy), sometimes plus SSI and CHIP. They work together. One program does not disqualify you from another. Stack them.
  • Medicaid picks up what private insurance doesn't pay. If you have private insurance plus Medicaid, use private insurance first, then Medicaid covers the gap. This is called coordination of benefits. It maximizes coverage and doesn't reduce what Medicaid pays.
  • Waiver eligibility is based on your child's own income, not family income. This is huge. Your family's income might be too high for SSI, but your child's own income (which starts at zero) might easily qualify for a Medicaid waiver. Waivers use SSI Federal Benefit Rate-based eligibility but evaluate income separately. Don't give up if your family income seems high.
  • School special education and Medicaid therapy are different services with different payers. School pays for educationally necessary services (IEP services). Medicaid (including EPSDT) pays for medical and therapeutic services. Both can fund speech or OT therapy for the same child because they serve different purposes. You don't choose; you access both.
  • IDEA eligibility does not automatically mean SSI eligibility, and vice versa. SSA uses its own evaluation standard (Blue Book functional limitations). Your child might be IDEA-eligible but not SSI-eligible, or the reverse. Apply to both and let each agency evaluate separately.
  • Part C (Early Intervention) eligibility does not automatically mean Part B (school) eligibility. Early Intervention has lower thresholds (developmental delay, as little as 25 percent in one area). School eligibility requires one of the 13 IDEA categories AND need for special education. Your child might age out of Part C and not qualify for Part B, or qualify for both. You still need separate evaluations at age 3.

Red Flags and Common Mistakes

  • Not applying because a screener said you don't qualify. You probably qualify. Screeners make mistakes. Apply anyway and let the formal evaluation happen. Denials are appealable.
  • Assuming your family income is too high for everything. Your family income might be too high for SSI but not Medicaid. Your child's own income might qualify for a Medicaid waiver. Your income might fit CHIP. Don't rule programs out based on what you think; apply and let income verification happen.
  • Not understanding Medicaid waiver waiting lists. Most states have them, and they're years long. Get on the list immediately, even if you don't need services now. You can decline services later if you don't need them.
  • Thinking SSI and SSDI are the same thing. They're not. SSI is based on the child's disability and low family income. SSDI is based on a parent's work history. Most families with children need SSI, not SSDI. Ask Social Security which one to apply for.
  • Thinking IDEA eligibility automatically means SSI eligibility. It doesn't. Your child might qualify for school services but not SSI cash benefits. SSA evaluates disability separately using the Blue Book standards (functional limitations). Apply to both and let each decide.
  • Not understanding that Section 504 is easier to access than IDEA. 504 covers any disability that substantially limits learning, self-care, hearing, seeing, etc. IDEA requires one of 13 categories plus need for special education. If you're not IDEA-eligible, you might still get 504 accommodations. Request both evaluations.
  • Not using ABLE accounts when available. These let you save money for your child without losing SSI or Medicaid benefits. Annual contribution limits (typically around 18K) and account balance limits (typically around 235K) change yearly. If your child qualifies for SSI, you should have an ABLE account.
  • Forgetting that EPSDT is a separate benefit even with standard Medicaid. If your child is on Medicaid, EPSDT services (dental, vision, hearing, developmental screening, therapy) are automatically available to kids under 21. Ask about this; many families don't know it exists.

Your Next Step

Go through the checklist above. For each program you might qualify for, follow the application link. Don't wait for a perfect diagnosis or full documentation; apply now. For state-specific details on eligibility, application timelines, and program details, see By State.
