Does My Child Qualify? (Probably For More Than You Think)

The Quick Answer

Your child probably qualifies for more benefits than you realize. Most kids with disabilities qualify for at least three or four programs. The trick is knowing which ones and how to apply. Here's a breakdown:

Program What It Is Who Qualifies Where to Apply
IDEA Part C (Early Intervention) Free developmental services (speech, OT, PT, developmental coaching) for kids 0–3. Paid by the state. Diagnosed disability or developmental delay (at least 25% delay in one area, 50% in another) State Early Intervention coordinator. Call your state health department.
IDEA Part B (School Special Education) Free special education services in school (resource room, self-contained classroom, inclusion support, therapies). Ages 3–21. Diagnosed disability affecting education: autism, Down syndrome, speech/language impairment, intellectual disability, other health impairment (OHI). Your local school district special education coordinator. Request evaluation in writing.
Section 504 Plan Accommodations (extra time on tests, modified assignments, accessible seating) without special education. Can also include medical procedures (feeding tube monitoring, medication administration). Any disability that substantially limits a major life activity (learning, self-care, hearing, seeing, concentrating). School district (usually the 504 coordinator or special education director). Request evaluation.
SSI (Supplemental Security Income) Monthly cash payment from Social Security. Amount varies by state and changes every year. Child disability (IDEA eligibility usually qualifies), family income and resources below federal limits that change annually. Check ssa.gov for current limits. ssa.gov or local Social Security office. Bring diagnosis letter, medical records, IEP.
Medicaid (Child) Health insurance covering doctor visits, therapy, hospitalization, prescription drugs, and preventive care including EPSDT. Low-income family (varies by state) OR child has disability and family qualifies (some states have expanded Medicaid for kids with disabilities regardless of income). Your state's Medicaid office (often combined with welfare/TANF office). Ask if you qualify. Don't assume you don't.
Medicaid Waiver (HCBS) Medicaid-funded long-term services at home/community: therapy, respite care, supported employment, residential support. Most states have waiting lists (often several years). Child qualifies for Medicaid. Disability requiring long-term support. Eligibility and waitlist vary by state and program. Your state's Medicaid office or disability services agency. Get on the waiting list immediately if eligible, even if you don't need services now.
CHIP (Children's Health Insurance Program) Health insurance for kids in families between Medicaid income limit and marketplace income. Covers similar services to Medicaid. Family income above Medicaid limit but below CHIP limit (usually 200–400% of federal poverty). No disability requirement. Your state's CHIP program (often integrated with Medicaid office) or healthcare.gov.
ABLE Account Tax-advantaged savings account for people with disabilities. Annual contribution limit and account balance limit (both change yearly with inflation). Check ablenrc.org for current limits and to open an account. Any person with a disability, regardless of income or family resources (if SSI-eligible or receiving disability benefits). ablenrc.org. Multiple ABLE account providers available.
Medicaid EPSDT Preventive health screenings, diagnosis, and treatment for kids on Medicaid. Covers dental, vision, hearing, developmental screening, therapy. Any child on Medicaid (under 21 in most states). Automatically available if child is on Medicaid. Ask your pediatrician or Medicaid office what EPSDT services are available in your state.

How to Apply for Multiple Programs

  1. Start with Early Intervention (if your child is 0–3). Call your state EI coordinator. It's free and takes just a phone call to request evaluation.
  2. Apply for Medicaid immediately. Even if you think you don't qualify, apply. Many families qualify for expanded Medicaid for kids with disabilities. Your state's website has the application, or call your Medicaid office.
  3. Get on the Medicaid waiver waiting list if available. Most states have waitlists, and some are years long. Get on the list now; you can use services later if you need them.
  4. Apply for SSI if your family income qualifies. Go to ssa.gov or your local Social Security office. Bring diagnosis documentation.
  5. Request school evaluation at age 3 (or earlier if you have concerns). Write your school district special education coordinator and request evaluation. Early special education is worth the effort.
  6. Open an ABLE account if your child has a disability and qualifies for SSI. You don't have to put money in it immediately, but having it open gives you a tool for saving without losing benefits.

You can qualify for so many programs that understanding them all requires a law degree and a spreadsheet. I made a spreadsheet. Then I lost it. Then I made another one.

Important Notes on Program Interactions

  • You don't have to choose between programs. Most families use Early Intervention AND school special education AND Medicaid (for therapy), sometimes plus SSI. They work together.
  • Medicaid picks up what insurance doesn't pay. If you have private insurance plus Medicaid, use private insurance first, then Medicaid covers the gap. This maximizes coverage.
  • Waiver eligibility is based on your child's income, not family income. This is huge. Your family's income might be too high for SSI, but your child's own income might be low enough for a waiver (which is based on SSI guidelines but evaluated separately).
  • School is required to provide services; you don't choose between school and medical Medicaid. Both happen. School pays for educationally necessary services; Medicaid pays for medical/therapeutic services. Both can fund therapy for the same child.

Red Flags and Common Mistakes

  • Not applying because you think you don't qualify. You probably do. Apply and let the agency decide.
  • Not understanding Medicaid waiver waiting lists. Most states have them, and they're years long. Get on the list immediately, even if you don't need services now.
  • Thinking SSI and SSDI are the same. They're not. Most families want SSI (child's disability), not SSDI (parents' work history).
  • Not using ABLE accounts when available. These let you save money for your child without losing benefits. That's valuable.

Your Next Step

Go through the checklist above. For each program you might qualify for, follow the application link. Don't wait for a perfect diagnosis or full documentation; apply now. For state-specific details on eligibility, application timelines, and program details, see By State.
